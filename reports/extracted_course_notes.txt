A brief history of information retrieval
• Information retrieval dates back to ancient libraries and archives.
• The 1800s saw punch cards for data processing, and in 1931, Goldberg invented the 
"Statistical Machine.”
• The field became a scientific discipline in the mid -20th century with contributions from 
Salton and Luhn.
• The 1960s saw the development of the SMART system, introducing key IR concepts.
• The 1970s brought probabilistic models and vector processing.
• The late 1990s saw IR systems widely adopted with the rise of search engines.
A brief history of information retrieval

Type of information retrieval models
• Boolean Model: Uses Boolean logic (AND, OR, NOT) for exact matching and ranking
• Vector Space Model: Represents documents and queries as vectors, using cosine similarity 
for ranking results based on relevance.
• Probabilistic Model: Estimates the probability of document relevance.
• Latent Semantic Indexing (LSI): Uses singular value decomposition (SVD) to capture 
semantic relationships and contextual meaning in text.

5). Handling special characters, numbers, and punctuation
• To remove punctuation and special characters from text data, we aim to clean the text and retain only alphanumeric 
characters and possibly spaces between words. 
• This step is essential in text preprocessing as punctuation marks and special characters often do not carry significant 
semantic meaning and can introduce noise in the data.
Why Do We Need to Handle Punctuation and Special Characters?
▪ Clarity: Punctuation helps convey meaning, but too much of it can confuse both readers and machines. For example, "I 
can't believe it's 5 o'clock!" vs. "I can’t believe it’s 5 o’clock!!"—the extra punctuation can change the tone and clarity 
of the message.
▪ Data Quality: In IR, we want our data to be clean and meaningful. Special characters (like #, $, %, etc.) often don’t add 
value to our analysis and can lead to misleading results.
▪ Efficiency: The more unnecessary characters (or "noise") we have in our data, the harder it is for algorithms to learn 
patterns. Cleaning up the text makes our models run faster and more accurately.

Bag of Words (BoW) models

• The Bag of Words (BoW) model is a fundamental and representation technique in Natural Language 
Processing (NLP) and Information Retrieval (IR) 
• The BoW model represents a document as an unordered set or “bag” of its words, ignore grammar 
and word order but considering the frequency of each word.
• The resulting representation is a vector where each element corresponds to a unique word in the 
vocabulary, and the value in each element reflects the frequency of that word in the document.

Bag of Words (BoW) models
• The basic idea behind Bag of Words is to simplify the complexity of language by converting a 
document into an unordered set of words, removing any information about the order in which 
the words appear and the grammatical structure of the sentences.

• To understand the importance of text preprocessing in BoW models, let’s look at a few key 
aspects:
A. Noise Reduction:
Remove punctuation, symbols, and irrelevant characters to keep only meaningful text.
B. Stop Word Removal:
Eliminate common words (like the, is, and) that add little meaning, reducing dimensionality.
C. Lowercasing:
Convert all text to lowercase to avoid treating words like Word and word as different.
D. Stemming & Lemmatization:
Normalize words to their root or dictionary form (e.g., running → run) to reduce vocabulary size 
and capture similar meanings.
Text Preprocessing Importance
• In BoW, each document uses only a few words from the total vocabulary, resulting in vectors mostly 
filled with zeros.
Advantages:
A. Computational Efficiency:
Operations skip zero values, reducing processing time - especially useful for large or high-dimensional 
datasets.
B. Memory Efficiency:
Only non-zero values are stored, saving memory and making it easier to handle large text collections.
Sparse Representation in Bag of Words (BoW)
A. Loss of Meaning:
BoW ignores word order and context, so it can’t understand the true meaning of phrases or sentences.
B. No Word Order:
It only counts words and doesn’t know the sequence they appear in, losing important context.
C. Document Length Issue:
Longer documents may seem more important just because they have more words, making comparisons 
harder .
D. High Dimensionality:
Large vocabularies create very big and sparse vectors, which can slow down processing and make 
models less efficient.
Challenges of Bag of Words
A. N-grams and Phrases:
Consider sequences of words (like bigrams or trigrams) to capture some word order and meaning.
B. TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency):
Weigh words based on importance - common words get lower weight, rare but meaningful words get 
higher weight - also helps with varying document lengths.
C. Word Embeddings (e.g., word2vec):
Use models like Skip-Gram or CBOW to capture semantic meaning and relationships between words for 
a richer representation.

BoW Lab
A. Bag of Words (BoW):
o  Represents a document as an unordered set of words, ignoring grammar and word order .
o Each word is independent, and the vector records word frequencies.
o Simple and frequency-based, but it cannot capture context or meaning between words.
B.  Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW):
o A word embedding model that predicts a target word using its surrounding context words.
o Uses a fixed-size context window and combines vectors of context words to learn the target word’s 
representation.
o Captures semantic meaning and relationships between words.
Similar but different BoW models
o Context Extraction: Slide a fixed-size window over text to collect surrounding words for each target 
word.
o Training Objective: Maximize the probability of predicting the target word from its context.
o Word Embeddings: Hidden layer weights become vector representations of words.
o Semantic Relationships: Words with similar meanings have similar vectors, enabling the model to 
understand relationships between words.
CBoW Work
A. Input Layer:
o Takes the context words within a fixed-size window around the target word.
B.  Hidden Layer:
o Functions as an embedding lookup layer, converting input words into dense vectors.
o The weights learned here capture semantic relationships between words.
C.  Output Layer:
o Predicts probabilities for each word in the vocabulary as the target word.
o Uses a softmax function to turn raw scores into probabilities.
